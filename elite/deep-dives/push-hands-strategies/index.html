Push Hands (Tui Shou) Strategies: From Form to Application¶

Why Push Hands Matters¶

If solo form practice is the classroom, Push Hands (Thôi Thủ 推手) is the laboratory. It is where everything you learn in form practice gets tested, refined, and made real. Without Push Hands, your Tai Chi remains theory. With it, you begin to understand the art from the inside.

This guide covers the strategic thinking behind Push Hands — the "why" behind the movements, not just the "how". Most students learn the techniques but miss the principles. This page helps you bridge that gap.

The Core Question: What Are You Training For?¶

Before any technique, you need to understand the purpose. Push Hands trains three things:

  1. Sensitivity (Ting Jin 听劲) — feeling your partner's intention, weight, and direction through contact
  2. Responsiveness (Hua Jin 化劲) — neutralizing incoming force without resisting
  3. Issuing (Fa Jin 发劲) — delivering your own force at the right moment, in the right direction

These three are the foundation. Everything else is application.

The Four Energies (Jin)¶

Mastery of Push Hands means understanding the four energies that flow through every interaction:

1. Peng Jin (掤劲) — Expansive Energy¶

The foundational energy of Tai Chi. Peng is the upward, outward expansion that maintains structure while remaining soft. It is not muscular rigidity — it is the energetic support of a balloon filled with water.

In Push Hands: When your partner pushes, you respond with Peng. You give slightly (so they don't meet hard resistance) but maintain structure (so they don't collapse you). It is the "give without giving way" quality.

Trainable quality: Can your partner feel a soft, springy quality at your contact point — like pressing on a fully inflated tire?

2. Lu Jin (捋劲) — Redirecting Energy¶

Lu is the energy of redirection. When force comes in, you don't meet it or block it — you turn it aside by moving your contact point along a curve.

In Push Hands: When partner pushes your wrist, you don't push back. You let their force travel down your arm while your hand guides it in a circular path. Their force is now moving past you, not into you.

Trainable quality: Can your partner feel their own force being redirected — and end up slightly off-balance without you having visibly "pushed" them?

3. Ji Jin (挤劲) — Compressing Energy¶

Ji is the energy of compression — pressing two surfaces together so the force has nowhere to go but through. Often used after Lu, when your partner's force is committed and moving past you, you close the gap and compress.

In Push Hands: After redirecting with Lu, you step in, close the angle, and press both palms toward your partner's center. Your arms are like the jaws of a vise closing.

Trainable quality: When you apply Ji, does your partner feel they cannot escape — that any direction they move is blocked?

4. An Jin (按劲) — Pressing/Settling Energy¶

An is the energy of downward pressing — the weight of your whole body dropping into your partner through your hands. Unlike Ji (which is horizontal compression), An is vertical settling.

In Push Hands: When your partner tries to rise or lift, you press down with the full weight of your body. This breaks their root and exposes their center.

Trainable quality: Does your partner feel a heavy, downward pressure that is impossible to push back up against?

The Five Strategies¶

Beyond the four energies, there are strategic principles that guide when to use each:

Strategy 1: Yield First, Then Respond¶

The biggest mistake beginners make is meeting force with force. The moment they feel pressure, they tense up and push back. This is the opposite of Tai Chi.

Principle: Let your partner's force arrive fully before responding. Don't try to intercept at the moment of contact. Let it come in, recognize its direction, and then respond with the appropriate energy.

Practical application: - Partner pushes your forearm - Wrong: You push back immediately, both arms locked - Right: Your arm softens slightly, their force travels past, your hand guides their arm in a circle, you then redirect or press

Strategy 2: Stick, Follow, Don't Break¶

In Push Hands, the contact between your arms and your partner's is called "sticking" (Zhan 粘). It should never break. If you lose contact, you've lost the conversation.

Principle: Maintain light contact at all times. Follow your partner's movements like a shadow. If they move their hand up, yours follows up. If they move down, yours follows down. You stay in dialogue.

Practical application: - Partner's hand moves in a small circle - Wrong: You pull away, then re-engage (you've broken the conversation) - Right: Your hand follows their circle, maintaining light, continuous contact

Strategy 3: Use the Opponent's Force¶

A master does not generate force to overcome force. They use what the opponent gives them.

Principle: Your partner's push contains direction, magnitude, and timing. You don't need to add your own force — you need to redirect theirs.

Practical application: - Partner pushes with 50 pounds of force toward your center - Wrong: You push back with 60 pounds - Right: You turn slightly, their 50 pounds now misses your center, you add 5 pounds of redirection, and they're off-balance

Strategy 4: Move from the Center, Not the Arms¶

Your hands and arms are like the tip of a whip — the force comes from the handle (your center).

Principle: Every movement in Push Hands originates from the Dantian (lower abdomen) and the waist. The arms are merely the delivery mechanism.

Practical application: - You're about to press your partner - Wrong: You push with your arms, your body stays still - Right: Your waist turns, your Dantian moves toward your partner, your arms transmit that movement — the force is whole-body

Strategy 5: Wait for the Right Moment¶

Issuing force at the wrong time wastes energy and reveals your intention.

Principle: Zhan, Nian, Lian, Sui (粘黏连随 — stick, adhere, connect, follow) until your partner is committed, off-balance, or has no escape — only then issue. Patience is power.

Practical application: - You're in contact with partner, both circling - Wrong: You push at every opportunity, exhausting yourself - Right: You follow and wait. You feel the moment their weight shifts to one foot, their structure opens, their attention wavers — then you issue, small but precise

Common Beginner Mistakes¶

Mistake 1: Arm Wrestling Mentality¶

Symptom: Both people pushing as hard as they can, no technique visible. Cause: Competitive instinct; treating Push Hands as a contest of strength. Fix: Remember the goal is sensitivity and responsiveness, not winning. Practice with a partner who agrees to work at 30% effort at first.

Mistake 2: Pulling Away¶

Symptom: Losing contact repeatedly, re-engaging with a bump. Cause: Fear of being pushed, instinctive retreat. Fix: Practice the single-hand circle until you can maintain contact for 5 minutes without breaking.

Mistake 3: Anticipating¶

Symptom: Partner hasn't moved yet, but you've already started your response. Cause: Trying to be one step ahead, but actually being one step late. Fix: Slow down. Wait for the actual movement before responding. Push Hands is reactive, not predictive.

Mistake 4: Using One Energy Only¶

Symptom: Every situation gets the same response (usually Peng or push). Cause: Not yet understanding the four energies as a vocabulary. Fix: Practice specific drills for each energy. Spend a month doing only Lu. Then a month doing only Ji. Build the vocabulary.

Mistake 5: Forgetting the Lower Body¶

Symptom: Hands moving, but feet stuck in place. Cause: Upper body disconnected from lower body. Fix: Step practice — every hand movement should be paired with a foot movement. Train stepping in patterns first, then add hands.

Training Progression¶

Stage 1: Single-Hand Circle (1-3 months)¶

Two partners, one hand each, maintaining contact and moving in slow circles. No intention to push, just maintain contact and follow.

Goal: Maintain unbroken contact for 10 minutes without thinking about it.

Stage 2: Four-Hand Circle (3-6 months)¶

Both hands in contact, both partners circling together. The classic "wading in water" pattern.

Goal: Feel the partner's weight shifting in all directions; respond with appropriate yielding.

Stage 3: Fixed-Step Push Hands (6-12 months)¶

Feet stay in bow stance (one forward, one back), upper body can move but lower body is fixed. Add Peng, Lu, Ji, An in sequence.

Goal: Smooth transitions between the four energies without losing contact or breaking structure.

Stage 4: Moving-Step Push Hands (12+ months)¶

Now feet can move. Add stepping, turning, evading. This is the full Push Hands.

Goal: Apply all four energies with movement; develop genuine responsiveness.

Stage 5: Free Push Hands (Years)¶

Open exploration. Any technique, any movement, with the goal of mutual learning rather than "winning".

Goal: Spontaneity. The techniques appear when needed without conscious thought.

A Note on Competition Push Hands¶

There is a competitive form of Push Hands (often seen in tournaments) that emphasizes throwing techniques. This is a separate discipline and uses different rules. The Push Hands described here is the traditional training form, focused on internal development, not competition.

Both have value, but they train different things. The traditional form trains sensitivity and softness. The competitive form trains timing and explosive power. Know which you are doing, and why.

A Closing Thought¶

The deepest lesson of Push Hands is not technical — it is relational. You cannot truly practice it alone. It requires another person, and it requires you to listen with your body, not just hear with your ears.

When you are in contact with a partner and you begin to feel their intention before they act, when your body responds without your mind having to think — that is Tai Chi becoming real.

慢 慢来 (màn màn lái) — slowly, slowly, you will arrive.
