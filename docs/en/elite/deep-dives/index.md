# Elite Deep Dives

## What Lives Here

The Elite deep-dives are the most advanced material in the Knowledge Base. They explore the mechanics, applications, and internal work that distinguish teacher-level practice from advanced practice.

These articles assume years of daily work, comfort with a long form, and meaningful partner practice. They are not introductions. They are the questions that surface when you've done the work and are ready to look at it more carefully.

I want to be honest: not every article here will make sense the first time you read it. Some won't make sense the tenth time. Internal development is layered, and these articles are written for practitioners who have the time and patience to return to them over years.

That's not a flaw. That's the point.

## Articles in This Section

### [Internal Mechanics: The Engineering of Internal Power](internal-mechanics/)

A detailed examination of how internal power actually works — physically, mechanically, energetically. The article covers the structural principles that make fajin possible, the body mechanics that allow ground reaction force to travel through bone rather than muscle, and the training progressions that develop this capacity over years.

The article is grounded in observable physics, not mystical claims. Every principle described can be tested with a partner and verified mechanically. At the same time, the article acknowledges what physics can't capture: the experiential quality of internal practice, and why that quality matters.

**Read this if:** you're serious about developing real internal power, and you want a mechanical understanding of how it works.

## How to Read These Articles

**Read them slowly.** These are not articles to consume. They're articles to study. Read a section, then put the article down. Practice what was described. Return the next day with the questions that came up.

**Read them more than once.** The first read is for orientation. The second is for understanding. The third is for noticing what you missed in the first two. Some of these articles reveal new content with each reading, because your practice has changed since last time.

**Practice between readings.** Reading about internal mechanics without practicing them is like reading about swimming without getting in the water. The text is the map. The practice is the territory. You need both.

**Test everything.** Nothing in these articles should be taken on faith. Every claim can be tested with a partner, with a mirror, or with honest self-observation. If a description doesn't match your experience, keep practicing. The experience will come.

## A Note on Authority

I've been practicing Tai Chi for over two decades. I've trained with teachers in Yang, Chen, and Wu styles. I've studied push hands with partners at every level, from beginner to national champion. I've taught hundreds of students, from complete beginners to advanced practitioners.

I'm telling you this not to claim authority, but to be transparent about perspective. What I write here is informed by all of that — and limited by it. There are traditions I haven't trained in. There are depth of practice I haven't reached. There are masters who would write these articles differently.

Take what resonates. Test what doesn't. Form your own understanding.

## What You'll Find in These Articles

- **Detailed mechanical analysis** — how internal principles work in terms of physics, anatomy, and structure
- **Training progressions** — how to develop these capacities step by step
- **Common errors** — what goes wrong, why, and how to correct it
- **Testing methods** — how to know if you're actually doing it right
- **Honest assessment** — when something doesn't work, the article says so

## What You Won't Find

- **Quick fixes.** Internal development takes years. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.
- **Magical thinking.** The principles in these articles are grounded in physics and anatomy. If something can't be tested mechanically, the article says so.
- **Style wars.** Yang, Chen, Wu, and Sun all have value. These articles focus on shared principles, not on which style is "best."
