# Ian Sinclair's Tai Chi Chuan Series

A four-part deep-dive into the teaching framework of Ian Sinclair, one of the clearest Western voices on traditional Yang-style Tai Chi. His work is unusual because it refuses to choose between two things most instructors separate: classical form and modern biomechanics, traditional principles and physical verification. This series pulls together his four major teaching threads so you can work through them in order, or pick the article that addresses whatever question brought you here today.

## Why This Series Exists

You have probably noticed that Tai Chi teaching splits into two camps. One camp talks in traditional language: energy, meridians, internal power, the language of a hundred years ago. The other camp talks in modern language: ground reaction force, joint stacking, moment arms, the language of sports science. Each camp tends to dismiss the other. The traditionalists say the biomechanists reduce Tai Chi to physics and miss the point. The biomechanists say the traditionalists are teaching mysticism dressed up as technique.

Ian Sinclair is one of the few teachers who refuses to take a side. His work keeps the traditional vocabulary because it is precise when you actually understand it. It keeps the biomechanical vocabulary because the body still has to obey gravity. The result is a teaching method that lets you test what you are doing in real time, without losing the depth that makes Tai Chi worth practicing in the first place.

If you have ever felt the gap between "the teacher told me to relax" and "I have no idea what to actually do with my shoulders right now," this series is for you.

## The Four Articles

Each article stands on its own, but they build on each other. Read them in order if you are new to Sinclair's work. Skip ahead if you have a specific question.

### 1. Classical Tai Chi Chuan — The Yang Family Framework

**[Ian Sinclair's Classical Tai Chi Chuan Series →](../ian-sinclair-classical/)**

Where to begin if you are new to Sinclair's teaching. This article covers the Classical Framework, the traditional Yang Chengfu lineage, and the foundational principles that the rest of his work assumes you know. It includes detailed form breakdowns with biomechanical precision, the integration of martial applications within health practice, and clear progressions from beginner to advanced levels.

Start here if you want to understand how Sinclair thinks about form.

### 2. Biomechanics — The Physical Foundation

**[Biomechanics of Tai Chi Movement: The Ian Sinclair Framework →](../ian-sinclair-biomechanics/)**

The article to read when you want to understand *why* a movement works, not just *how* to do it. This piece grounds Tai Chi mechanics in observable physical principles: structure, weight transfer, ground reaction forces, and whole-body coordination. It is the bridge between traditional vocabulary and modern sports science.

The practical test method Sinclair uses throughout — partner pressure, balance challenges, structural alignment checks — is fully developed here.

### 3. Mechanics & Principles — The Internal Force

**[Ian Sinclair's Mechanics & Principles Series →](../ian-sinclair-mechanics/)**

The article on jin (internal force). This is where the conversation shifts from external form to internal development: how the body generates whole-body power without muscular tension, how the waist directs movement, how the legs root force through the ground. It is the most demanding article in the series, both physically and conceptually.

Read this once you have been practicing for a while and want to understand what "internal" actually means beyond the slogan.

### 4. Advanced Applications — Form Meets Martial

**[Ian Sinclair's Advanced Applications Series →](../ian-sinclair-advanced/)**

The article where health practice and martial application converge. Push hands skills, fa jin (power issuance), sensitivity development, and the martial interpretation of form movements. This is the longest article in the series because it covers the most ground — the meeting point of everything Sinclair teaches.

Save this for last unless you are specifically interested in the martial side of the practice.

## How To Use This Series

If you are a beginner, start with the Classical article. Work through the form breakdowns. Don't rush to the internal mechanics — you need a foundation in the form before the internal work makes sense.

If you are an intermediate practitioner with form experience, start with Biomechanics. It will fill in the *why* behind what you already do. Then move to Mechanics when you are ready.

If you are an advanced practitioner, you can read any of the four in any order. The Advanced Applications article will probably be the most useful, because it pulls together everything from the other three.

If you are a teacher, the Biomechanics and Mechanics articles will give you clearer language for principles you may have been struggling to articulate.

## What This Series Is Not

This is not a beginner's introduction to Tai Chi. For that, start with the [Foundation Basics](../../basics/) page. The Ian Sinclair series assumes you already know what Tai Chi is, that you have some form experience, and that you are ready to think more carefully about what you are doing.

This is also not a complete translation of Sinclair's full body of work. These four articles pull the most important threads from his teaching into one place, but his full curriculum is much larger. If you want the complete picture, look for his books and his YouTube channel after you have read the articles here.
