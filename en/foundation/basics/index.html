    Foundation Basics¶

    What This Section Is¶

    This is the entry point. Everything else in the Knowledge Base — Advanced, Elite, all the deep-dives — assumes you've done the work described here.

    The Foundation isn't a warm-up. It isn't something you "graduate from" and never return to. The masters I respect most spend half their practice time on what you'll find below. There is no shortcut, and there is no finishing line. The basics reveal new layers as your body learns to feel them.

    I get asked all the time: "I've been practicing for years. Do I really need to review the basics?" Yes. Every time, without exception. Not because you're doing it wrong, but because basics are layered. What you couldn't feel a year ago, you feel clearly now. What you can feel now, you'll feel differently in another year.

    The Core Work¶

    1. Stance and Posture¶

    The foundation of everything is how you stand. Without good stance, every movement above it carries tension you don't know about.

    Wuji Stance (無極樁): Feet hip-width apart, knees soft, spine neutral, head suspended, arms relaxed at your sides. Stand for 1-3 minutes to start. The point isn't the duration — it's the noticing. Feel your weight in your feet. Feel your breath. Notice where you're holding.

    Bow Stance (弓步, Gong Bu): Front foot forward, knee over ankle (never past toes), back leg straight, weight roughly 60/40. The classic "warrior" stance. Get used to holding it for a minute at a time. Feel the load in the front leg. Feel the back leg rooting.

    Cat Stance (虛步, Xu Bu): Front foot just touching, weight on the back leg. The opposite of bow stance. Trains the empty leg to stay light, ready to step. Most beginners grip the floor with the front foot. Let it barely touch.

    Empty Stance: A preparation stance for movement. Weight is fully on one leg. The other is relaxed, ready. Practice shifting into and out of empty stance smoothly.

    2. Weight Transfer¶

    Tai Chi is the art of moving weight without falling over. Sounds obvious. It isn't.

    Shift and Step: From a 50/50 stance, shift your weight fully to one leg, then step with the other. The step should feel weightless. If you can hear your foot hit the floor, you're carrying too much weight into the step.

    Rock and Roll: A subtle weight shift from one foot to the other, side to side. The motion should originate in the waist, not the feet. The feet just follow.

    Single Leg Balance: Stand on one leg for 30 seconds to start, working up to 2 minutes. Eyes open first, then closed. This is balance training that transfers directly to your form.

    3. Basic Arm Energies¶

    The four main energies of Tai Chi. You'll find these throughout every form, every style.

    Ward Off (掤, Peng): Expansive, lifting, like holding a large ball. The arm is curved, the structure is connected. Press into a Ward Off arm — it doesn't collapse. It pushes back through structure, not muscle.

    Rollback (履, Lu): Yielding, redirecting. The hands move in a circle, drawing force past you rather than meeting it. This is the energy of "yes, and" — accepting what comes and guiding it somewhere.

    Press (擠, Ji): Forward intention with the back hand supporting the front. The power comes from the legs and waist, not the arms. Think of it as advancing your whole body, with the arms just being the contact point.

    Push (按, An): Downward then forward, both hands working together. Roots you to the ground while extending outward. Used for closing energy, for finishing an interaction.

    4. Single Movements¶

    Before learning a full form, work on individual movements. This is how you develop quality.

    • Grasp Bird's Tail (摟雀尾): Ward Off, Rollback, Press, Push — the four energies in one sequence. The single most important movement in Yang style. Master this and you have a foundation.
    • Wave Hands Like Clouds (雲手): Continuous lateral movement driven by the waist. The arms follow; the waist leads. The eyes follow the leading hand.
    • Single Whip (單鞭): One arm extends out as a "hook" while the other pushes. Trains the asymmetric structure that's fundamental to Tai Chi.
    • White Crane Spreads Wings (白鶴亮翅): Single-leg balance with one arm raised. Trains the empty leg, the lifted arm, the integrated whole.
    • Brush Knee and Push (摟膝拗步): The hand brushes past the knee as the body steps forward. Trains hand-foot coordination and forward intention.

    Practice Guidelines¶

    Frequency beats duration. Ten minutes a day is better than ninety minutes once a week. Daily practice, even short, builds the habit. The habit is what matters.

    Slow is smooth, smooth is fast. Move slowly enough that you can feel what's happening. Speed is the last thing to develop, not the first. The slow practice of today is the foundation of the fast practice of ten years from now.

    One correction at a time. Don't try to fix everything in one session. Today, work on posture. Tomorrow, breathing. Next week, weight transfer. Layer the corrections slowly. If you try to fix three things at once, you'll fix none of them.

    Film yourself monthly. What you think you're doing and what you're actually doing are usually different. Set your phone up, record 30 seconds of a basic movement, and watch it back. Compare to the instruction videos. Note one thing to improve. Do that for a year. You'll be unrecognizable.

    Find a teacher if you can. The articles here are maps. A teacher is a guide who can correct your structure in real time. Books and videos will take you far, but in-person instruction goes further. Even occasional workshops — a weekend every few months — are valuable.

    Common Beginner Questions¶

    "How long until I can do the form?" Depends on the form. A simple 12-movement form: 3-6 months. The 24-Form: 6-12 months. A 108-movement long form: 1-2 years. And "knowing the form" isn't the goal. The goal is doing the form well. That takes longer.

    "Should I learn a form first or push hands first?" Form. Always form first. Push hands requires a partner, and it requires a foundation that you build through solo practice. Trying to learn push hands before you can do the form is like trying to have a conversation before you know the words.

    "Is Tai Chi exercise or martial art?" Both. The same movements serve both purposes. The health benefits are well-documented. The martial applications are real, but they require years of practice to develop. Don't choose one or the other. They reinforce each other.

    "What if I'm too old / stiff / out of shape?" Tai Chi is for every body. It was developed by people who wanted to maintain health and longevity. Modifications exist for every physical limitation. Start where you are. The form adapts to you; you don't have to force yourself into it.
