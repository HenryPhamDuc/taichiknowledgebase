    Advanced Deep Dives¶

    What Lives Here¶

    Foundation gives you the movements. Advanced gives them meaning. This section is where the form stops being choreography and starts being a martial art, an internal practice, and a way of moving through the world.

    Each deep-dive article here takes one specific aspect of Advanced practice and explores it in depth — the principles behind it, the common errors in learning it, and the practices that build it.

    These are not introductory articles. They assume you can already do the movements and you're asking the harder question: why do they work, and how do I get better at them?

    Articles in This Section¶

    Martial Applications: What Each Movement Really Does¶

    Every Tai Chi movement has a martial meaning — usually hidden. This article reveals the applications behind Ward Off, Roll Back, Press, Push, and the other foundational techniques. You learn how each movement works as self-defense, why it looks the way it does, and how to test it with a partner.

    Read this if: you've wondered whether Tai Chi "actually works" as a martial art, or you've been told it does but no one could show you how.

    Internal vs. External Power Generation¶

    External power is what most martial artists develop — muscular force, generated locally and pushed outward. Internal power (often called fajin, 發勁) is something different: force that arises from the whole body, transmitted through structure rather than muscle.

    This deep-dive compares the two in detail. You'll learn how to recognize which kind you're using, why internal power is more efficient, and the specific training that develops it. The article also addresses the elephant in the room: is internal power real, or is it a cultural myth?

    Read this if: you want to develop power that doesn't depend on strength, age, or athletic ability.

    How to Read These Articles¶

    Don't skim. These are deep-dives for a reason. Each one builds a layered argument, and the conclusions only hold up if you follow the reasoning.

    Practice while you read. Read a section, then put the article down and try what it describes. Return to the text with the question that came up in your body. Internal arts are learned in the body, not in the head.

    Test with a partner. Wherever the article suggests a partner exercise, find someone willing to do it. Without a partner, you'll be guessing at half of what's described. Push hands and partner work are not optional extras — they're how you verify the principles.

    Return to the articles. What you don't understand today will become obvious in a year. The same article read three times over three years reveals three different layers. Internal development is layered.

    A Note on Skepticism¶

    You will encounter claims in these articles that sound like nonsense the first time you hear them. "Move from your center." "Use the ground." "Let your opponent's force return to them." These phrases mean something precise, but the precision is felt, not heard.

    When something doesn't make sense, set it aside. Keep practicing. Come back to the article in a few months. The same sentence that read like mystical nonsense will one day read like a precise technical description of what your body just did.

    This is normal. Internal arts are learned by doing, not by understanding them intellectually. The articles are maps. The territory is your practice.
